# Survey and Practical Evaluation of Positioning Devices

We conducted a series of surveys and practical experiments on several commercially available devices that claim to support Bluetooth Angle-of-Arrival (AoA) development:

## Positioning Device 1  
The *White Paper on High-Precision Bluetooth AoA Positioning Technology* states that the TLSR825x and TLSR827x series of Bluetooth chips support AoA/AoD functionality.  

We purchased a TLSR8278 development kit and followed the instructions provided at http://wiki.telink-semi.cn/wiki/chip-series/TLSR827x-Series/ to extract AoA information.  

However, during experimentation, the acquired AoA values remained constant—no valid AoA data could be successfully obtained. Moreover, this development platform does not expose Bluetooth phase information, rendering it unsuitable for custom development.

## Positioning Device 2  
Texas Instruments (TI) offers a Bluetooth positioning system comprising three node types: Master, Passive, and Slave—corresponding respectively to the three BLE communication roles. The Slave broadcasts Bluetooth packets containing Constant Tone Extension (CTE) upon command from the Master; both the Master and Passive nodes are equipped with antenna arrays to receive these broadcast packets and compute AoA.  

This system supports one Master communicating with multiple Slaves and multiple Passives. However, due to BLE protocol constraints, it does not support multiple Masters within a single positioning scenario—i.e., a Slave cannot simultaneously connect to multiple Masters.

The system leverages the Constant Tone Extension (CTE) mechanism introduced in the Bluetooth 5.1 specification. CTE appends a sequence of consecutive 0s or 1s to the end of a packet, effectively transmitting a continuous-tone sinusoidal signal at a fixed frequency. This enables AoA estimation analogous to other antenna-array-based localization methods: phase differences across the antenna array are converted into path-length differences; combined with known inter-antenna geometry and distances, the angle of arrival (AoA) is computed.

The provided AoA antenna array consists of three linearly arranged antennas, capable of resolving AoA within a 180° angular range—but unable to distinguish whether the signal arrives from the front or rear side of the array. Experimental observations indicate higher localization accuracy near the center of this 180° range, with noticeable degradation as angles approach either extreme. Therefore, for optimal performance, it is recommended to deploy these antenna arrays around the perimeter of the target environment, oriented toward the most probable locations of the device under test. Additionally, multipath propagation significantly degrades positioning accuracy; thus, mounting the device on a tripod (i.e., elevated above ground or desktop surfaces) yields substantially improved accuracy compared to surface placement.

Unlike conventional simultaneous-sampling antenna arrays, this AoA antenna array employs time-division (sequential) sampling. Upon arrival of each packet, the current antenna receives for 8 ms, after which sampling rotates among the antennas. Each antenna samples for 1–2 ms, followed by a 1–2 ms switch slot—a transition interval required for antenna switching. Critically, all samples acquired during this transition interval are retained; consequently, raw data exhibits smoothly varying inter-antenna phase differences rather than abrupt jumps. During its dedicated sampling window, each antenna measures I/Q values, from which the instantaneous phase can be derived.
